Do richer schools really have better tech? It depends what you ask.

PISA 2025, up to 91 education systems. We looked at seven measures of school computer/device resourcing and checked, for each one, whether richer schools, city schools and private schools are actually ahead — or whether the gap runs the other way.

Seven resources, three ways schools differ

Each row is one resource; each column is one way of splitting schools. Blue means richer / city / private schools are better off on average, which is what you'd usually expect (for the two shortage rows, that means fewer reported problems). Red means the opposite: poorer, rural or public schools are better off. Grey means no statistically clear average gap. Tap or click any box for the full story in that one cell.

What stands out

  • Private vs public is the clearest divide. On both "lack of resources" and "poor-quality resources", private schools are better off by about 12 percentage points on average. In 51 of 79 countries the gap clearly favours private schools, and in none does it clearly favour public ones.
  • Richer schools report fewer problems too. The gap is about 9 to 10 percentage points on average, and it clearly favours richer schools in 40 to 45 of 89 countries, against only 2 or 3 the other way.
  • Raw device counts tell a different story. For computers, poorer schools are clearly ahead in 29 countries and richer schools in 17. Rural schools are clearly ahead in 28 countries and city schools in only 3. Because these are per-student counts, a small school can look well supplied without having more in total, and the OECD notes the pattern may partly reflect that.
  • Readiness to teach digitally leans towards richer and private schools. The average gap is modest (about 0.2 to 0.35 index points) but very consistent: richer schools clearly ahead in 47 of 90 countries versus 6 the other way, private schools in 48 of 79 versus 4.
  • Location matters least for quality. City vs rural gaps on shortages and poor quality are not statistically clear on average, and in %%LOC_NOCLEAR%% countries there's no clear difference on either measure.
  • Teacher connectivity is mixed. Average gaps are small or unclear, yet richer schools are clearly ahead in more countries than behind (32 vs 9 by socio-economic profile, 25 vs 12 by school type), while by location countries split 12 vs 11. E-readers are so rare that their gaps hardly matter.

"Clearly" means the difference is larger than sampling error would explain (about 95% confidence). Country counts come from testing each country separately.

Which countries buck the usual pattern?

For each country we can test up to 21 gaps (7 resources × 3 ways of splitting schools). This ranks countries by how many of their statistically clear gaps go against the usual "richer schools have more" pattern.
